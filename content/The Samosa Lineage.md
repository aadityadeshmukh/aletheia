# The Samosa Lineage: From Royalty to the Street

The modern samosa, a staple of global street food, is an evolved descendant of an aristocratic Persian past. As chronicled in the **Ni'matnama** (the 16th-century "Book of Delights"), the original iteration was a perfumed, ghee-fried delicacy for the Sultans of Mandu—devoid of the potatoes and chilies that would later define its identity following the Columbian Exchange. Its journey from the Mughal court to the street stall is a masterclass in the democratization of taste and the resilience of culinary form.

### Analysis
- **The Pre-Columbian Plate:** Before the arrival of New World ingredients, the samosa relied on roasted aubergine, dried ginger, and dry-cooked lamb, emphasizing aromatic complexity over the capsaicin heat of modern versions.
- **Historical Transit:** The Ni'matnama's passage through the hands of the Mughal Emperor Akbar, Tipu Sultan, and the East India Company mirrors the political and cultural shifts that shaped the Indian subcontinent itself.
- **The Royal-to-Street Path:** The democratization of the samosa highlights how luxury items can be stripped of their expensive ingredients (meat) and repurposed into mass-market staples (potato) without losing their fundamental structural appeal.

### Synthesis
Culinary history is a map of global trade and cultural migration. **The samosa is a fossil record of the transition from the courtly to the common.**
